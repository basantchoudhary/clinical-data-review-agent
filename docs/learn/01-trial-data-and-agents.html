How trial data gets clean, and where an agent fits

You already know what an agent is. This lesson is about the domain: how a clinical trial's data is collected and cleaned, which parts of that work are rules, and which part is judgement. By the end you should be able to say, in your own words, why this project needs an agent and why most of it still should not be one.

The questions this lesson answers
  1. How does a patient's visit become rows in a database?
  2. Who cleans trial data today, and what do they actually do all day?
  3. Which checks are simple rules, and which need someone to think?
  4. What does that thinking look like on one real patient record?
  5. So where exactly does an agent belong, and where must it not go?

1. A trial in one picture

A trial runs at many hospitals, called sites. Each patient comes to the site for a series of visits: screening, week 2, week 4 and so on. At each visit, site staff fill in electronic forms called eCRFs (case report forms) in an EDC system, the trial's data-entry database. Our EDC is OpenClinica.

The loop in blue is the heart of data cleaning. Someone at the sponsor or CRO notices something odd and raises a query: a short, polite question on a specific field. In OpenClinica a query is called a discrepancy note. The site checks the patient's source notes and either corrects the data or explains it. Only when every query is closed is the database locked, then converted to SDTM, the standard layout regulators expect.

The data you have is already SDTM. That is fine: SDTM keeps the same facts, arranged as one table per topic (a domain). We will load it back into OpenClinica as forms, so the agent works against a real EDC the way it would at a sponsor.

DomainWhat it holdsIn your pilot data
DM demographicsOne row per patient: age, sex, site, treatment arm, consent and study dates306 patients at 17 sites; 3 arms (placebo, low dose, high dose) plus 52 screen failures
SV subject visitsWhich visits happened, and whenAbout 12 visits per patient
AE adverse eventsAnything medically bad that happened, with severity, seriousness and the investigator's view of whether the drug caused it1,191 events; the most common are itching and redness where the patch was applied

The pilot study is a public test dataset from CDISC and PHUSE (an Alzheimer's trial of xanomeline, given as a skin patch). Treat it as realistic, not as real people.

2. Who cleans the data, and how

Two kinds of checking happen, and the difference between them is the whole design of this project.

Edit checksData review
WhoThe EDC itself, built by a study programmerData managers and a medical monitor (a doctor at the sponsor)
Looks atOne field or one formThe whole patient, across forms and visits
Example"Age must be between 50 and 95." "Visit date cannot be before consent." "ALT is above the normal range, so flag it.""ALT is three times normal and bilirubin is rising, yet no liver event is recorded. Has the investigator assessed this?"
NatureDeterministic same input, same answer, written in advanceJudgement what to look at next depends on what you just found
Right toolCode. In our build, data-quality rules in your Databricks pipeline.This is the only place an agent belongs.

Data review is slow, repetitive and expensive. A reviewer opens a patient, reads across six or seven domains, and decides what is worth asking. Most patients are fine. Finding the few that are not, and asking the right question, is the skill.

3. One real patient: 01-705-1186

This is a patient from your pilot data. Read it the way a reviewer would. Notice that each step decides what you look at next.

1

The edit check fires. ALT at week 2 is 104 against an upper limit of 32: about 3.3 times normal. A rule can see this. It always will. rule

2

So the reviewer opens the adverse events. There is one: hyperbilirubinaemia, moderate, not serious, "remote" relation to the drug, starting 26 Jan. Nothing about ALT or AST. Is a transaminase rise this large an event the investigator should assess? judgement

3

That sends them to bilirubin. It jumped to about 5.5 times normal on 23 Jan, three days before the recorded start of the event. Is the onset date right? judgement

4

ALT above 3× with bilirubin above 2× rings a bell: it is the pattern regulators watch for as possible drug-induced liver injury (often called Hy's law). But a careful reviewer checks one more thing: ALP was already about 5 times normal at screening, before any study drug. The patient's medical history records Paget's disease of bone, which raises ALP from bone rather than liver, so ALP alone tells us little about the liver here. The rise in ALT, AST and bilirubin after dosing still needs the investigator's assessment. So the right move is to ask, not to declare anything.

Correction, 28 Oct: an earlier version said the high ALP pointed to a blocked bile duct. The agent's first real run found the Paget's disease in the medical history, which is the better explanation. A good example of why the agent asks rather than diagnoses, and why reviewers read the whole record. judgement

5

The screening values raise their own question. Liver labs were already high before the patient started. Did the protocol's entry criteria allow that? The reviewer opens the protocol's eligibility list. judgement, a different source

6

Finally, disposition. The patient left on 7 Feb. The reason is recorded as physician decision, with the text "hyperbilirubinemia". If an adverse event was the reason, should the reason say adverse event? judgement

Here is what that review could produce: drafts only, for a data manager to approve and send.

Query 1 · AE form. ALT and AST were above 3× the upper limit of normal from 23 Jan (ALT 104 U/L, ULN 32). No adverse event for raised transaminases is recorded. Please confirm whether the investigator assessed this as an adverse event, and if so add it.
Query 2 · AE form, hyperbilirubinaemia. Total bilirubin was above 5× ULN on 23 Jan; the event's start date is 26 Jan. Please confirm the onset date.
Query 3 · Disposition form. The reason for discontinuation is "physician decision" with the term "hyperbilirubinemia". Please confirm whether the reason should be recorded as adverse event.
Query 4 · Eligibility. ALT, AST, ALP and bilirubin were above the normal range at screening (ALP 565 U/L, ULN 115). Please confirm the patient met the protocol's laboratory entry criteria.

Notice what the agent is not doing. It does not decide whether this is liver injury. It does not change any data. It does not send anything. It finds, cites the exact records, and asks. The investigator, a doctor, answers.

4. So, is this agentic?

Use the same four tests on any idea. All four must hold before an agent earns its place.

TestPatient 01-705-1186Result
Is the path unknown in advance?Lab, then adverse events, then the onset date, then the liver-injury pattern, then screening and the protocol, then disposition. Another patient would take a different path.yes
Does it need several sources?Five domains plus the protocol documentyes
Is judgement the hard part?Step 4 is the clearest case: knowing when the pattern applies and when ALP changes the pictureyes
Can mistakes be caught?A data manager approves every query before it goes to the siteyes

Now the important part for an architect: most of the system is still not an agent.

PieceWhat it isWhy
Loading the data and building SDTM-aligned layersPipeline your Databricks codeSame every time; must be reproducible and auditable
Edit checks (out of range, missing, impossible dates)Rules data-quality expectationsCheaper, exact, explainable to an auditor
Choosing which patients to review firstRules a score from the rule hitsNo judgement needed to rank
Reviewing one patient across forms and drafting queriesAgent Claude with narrow, read-only toolsPasses all four tests
Approving and sending a queryPersonAccountability stays with a human

This split, rules first and the agent only where rules cannot reach, is the first architecture decision you will make. Being able to explain it is worth more in an interview than the agent itself.

5. Check your understanding

Try to answer before opening each one. Bring anything that feels unclear to the chat.

Why can't an edit check write Query 1?

An edit check can see that ALT is high. It cannot know whether the investigator already judged it not clinically significant, whether another event covers it, or whether this patient's history explains it. Deciding that a question is worth asking needs the whole record.

Why does the agent ask instead of saying "this is drug-induced liver injury"?

Because it is not its decision, and the record is more complicated than one pattern: ALP was high from screening, and the medical history shows Paget's disease of bone, a non-liver cause. Clinical judgement belongs to the investigator. The agent's job is to find and ask, with the evidence attached.

This patient was on placebo. Does that make the queries pointless?

No. The trial is blinded: neither the site nor the reviewer knows the arm while it runs. Every patient is reviewed the same way. And the data must be right regardless of arm.

Name one thing in this project that should never become agentic.

Any of: loading and transforming the data, the edit checks, ranking patients, or sending a query. The first three are deterministic; the last is accountability.
